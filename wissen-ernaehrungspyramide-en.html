National Dietary Guidelines Compared Internationally: What the Latest Research from September 2026 Says

One day eggs are good, the next day they are bad, and then carbohydrates are the problem. A new international pyramid shows that at their core, the guidelines agree surprisingly well. We look at what it says and where its limits lie.

Patrick SpenglerCertified Nutrition Coach
November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

01What this is about

Sound familiar? You read that eggs are healthy. A week later you are supposed to avoid them. Sometimes carbohydrates are the problem, sometimes fat, sometimes everything at once. In the end you stand in the supermarket and no longer know what should go into the cart.

That is unsettling, and it is completely normal. The good news: when experts from different countries lay their recommendations side by side, they agree surprisingly well at their core. And in everyday life, small adjustments are often enough, with no bans at all.

That is exactly what an international team set out to do. The International Carbohydrate Quality Consortium, an alliance of nutrition researchers, published an “International Food Guide Pyramid” in the journal Advances in Nutrition in September 2026. 23 researchers took part, including Anette Buyken of Paderborn University from Germany. According to a press release from Brigham Young University, there had been no international pyramid that brings such recommendations together until now.

The short version

  • Guidelines from Canada, the USA, Northern Europe, Germany, the Netherlands, China and Australia agree at their core: lots of plant foods, unsaturated rather than saturated fats, and red and processed meat rather rarely.
  • The quality of carbohydrates is emphasized in particular: whole grains, fiber and foods with a rather low glycemic index instead of finely milled starch and sweetened drinks.
  • The pyramid is a consensus paper, not a new study. It gives orientation for healthy people, but not a personal plan. Several of those involved disclose ties to the food industry.

02What the science says

How the pyramid came about

The team compared guidelines from Canada, the USA (2025 advisory committee report), the Nordic countries, Germany, the Netherlands, China and Australia. For the relative amounts, they used the reference diet of the EAT-Lancet Commission, which assumes about 2,400 kcal per day. Existing reviews and selected individual studies were added. The paper lists 292 references.

An important note: this is a summary work, not a new study. The recommendations came out of discussion within the team, without a vote and without a formal assessment of the strength of the evidence. The authors themselves call their approach “evidence-informed”, meaning guided by the evidence, but not “evidence-based” in the strict sense.

The international recommendation: the food pyramid

  • Base: vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains and traditional grain products with a low glycemic index. At least half of the grains should be whole grain.
  • Plant protein sources: legumes, nuts and seeds. The guide value from the EAT-Lancet reference diet is 125 g per day (dry weight), which corresponds to about 3 to 5 servings.
  • Animal foods in moderation: at most about two servings per day. That is one dairy serving (250 g yogurt or milk, preferably fermented, zero is also possible) and at most one further serving (75 to 100 g fish, poultry or eggs, or a little lean red meat).
  • Red meat: The guide value is about 14 g per day. A calculation example, not a study result: that is roughly 100 g per week (14 g times 7 days = 98 g).
  • Fats: olive oil, rapeseed oil and other vegetable oils instead of butter, lard and tallow.
  • Rarely: refined grains, red and processed meat, chips, sweets, sweetened drinks and a lot of salt.

Carbohydrates: quality, not just quantity

What is special about this pyramid is its focus on the quality of carbohydrates. Besides whole grain, the glycemic index (GI) also counts. It describes how strongly a food raises blood sugar. According to the paper, some foods without whole grain also have a low GI, for example durum wheat pasta, basmati or parboiled rice and bulgur, because their structure stays intact longer during digestion.

For bread, the paper distinguishes more precisely than many news reports did: according to the paper, both white bread and bread made from finely milled whole grain flour lead to higher blood sugar responses, because the grain structure is destroyed during milling. It names bread with whole kernels as more favorable. The pyramid rates sugar by the form of the food and singles out sweetened drinks.

How robust is this?

For carbohydrate quality, the authors rely on cohort studies that link lower GI values with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, and on meta-analyses of randomized trials on risk factors such as blood sugar and blood lipids. Two things to keep in mind: cohort studies show associations, not a clear cause. And risk factors are not the same as diseases.

In the same journal, Lukas Schwingshackl (University of Freiburg) and Sabrina Schlesinger (German Diabetes Center) comment on the pyramid. They see the approach of swapping foods and the focus on carbohydrate quality as strengths and the pyramid as an important contribution. They point out, however, that guidelines mostly rest on observational studies, which can be distorted by confounding factors and measurement error, and that no formal procedure was used to assess the strength of the evidence. In addition, members of the consortium disclose financial relationships with the food industry. That does not mean the recommendations are wrong. But it is a reason to read individual statements with a sense of proportion. The paper itself names no funding.

For perspective: what the pyramid does not say

  • It is aimed at healthy people aged 2 and older. Other recommendations are needed for illness, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
  • It shows proportions, not fixed portions for you. Energy needs are individual.
  • It is not a guide to losing weight.
  • It describes healthy, varied eating and is not sports nutrition. Training, competition or targeted muscle building can call for different adjustments.

On protein, the paper considers 0.8 g per kg of body weight sufficient for most adults and mentions that somewhat more protein could support muscle mass during weight loss and in older age. More on this in our article on protein.

According to the paper, anyone who eats few or no animal foods should pay attention to vitamin B12. What suits you is best clarified with a doctor.

03How you could put it into practice

The pyramid describes a pattern, not a program. Every body, every daily routine and every history is different. What suits you can only be looked at individually. Here are a few ideas that could be easy to build into everyday life:

  • Whole grain If it fits into your day, you could swap part of your grains, for example oats for breakfast or whole grain rice at lunch. As a guide value, the paper names at least half.
  • Bread If you like bread, a loaf with whole kernels can be an option.
  • Legumes Lentils, chickpeas or beans as a stew, dal or hummus. One more meal per week can already make a difference.
  • Fat For cooking, olive or rapeseed oil can be a good choice.
  • Drinks Water or unsweetened tea instead of sweetened drinks, if that comes easily to you.
  • Pasta, rice & co. Combined with vegetables, legumes or fish, they are part of a balanced plate.

None of this is a ban. Butter, meat and sweets can still be part of your diet. How that fits together is described in “The Dose Makes the Poison”. You will find more on carbohydrates in “Carbs: Do They Really Make You Fat?”.

Your starting point: Pick one or two things that feel easy. For example one meal per week with legumes, and oats instead of white flour muesli on your next shop.

If you have existing conditions such as diabetes or kidney disease, individual recommendations apply and should be set by a doctor.

The ASCENSUS View

What I like most about the pyramid is one thing: it does not say “never eat X again”, it says “do more of Y”. That fits the way I work.

Guidelines are orientation, not dogma. They describe what holds on average for healthy and varied eating, and they do not replace my looking at the individual person. A pyramid does not know your everyday life. It does not know when you finish work, whether you cook or are on the road a lot, and what you really like to eat.

On top of that, this is about healthy eating, not sports nutrition. If you train, work toward a competition or want to build muscle on purpose, your requirements are often quite different, and they do not necessarily have anything to do with healthy everyday eating. Then, for example, more than the 0.8 g of protein per kg of body weight can make sense, and a very high-fiber meal right before a long run is more of a hindrance for many people. Such adjustments are deliberate tools that have to fit the goal.
