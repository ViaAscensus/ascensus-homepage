Gut and Microbiome: What You Can Really Influence

A moody belly, new gut trends all the time and powders on every shelf: what is behind it? We sort out what the studies show and what you can try without a cure.

Patrick SpenglerCertified Nutrition Coach
November 24, 2026 · 6 min read

01What this is about

Does your belly press, bloat or rumble after eating, and you wonder whether it is down to your diet? Digestive complaints have many causes, and diet is only one of them. If they occur more often, get stronger or worry you, please have them checked by a doctor. This article does not replace that. It is aimed at healthy adults. If you have a known bowel condition such as irritable bowel syndrome, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis or celiac disease, a weakened immune system, or are pregnant, please discuss changes to your diet with your doctor.

At the same time: an ecosystem of bacteria lives in your gut. It lives on what you eat, above all on fiber from vegetables, fruit, legumes and whole grains. From this fiber, the bacteria make substances that nourish your gut wall and exchange signals with your immune system.

Why this matters: the clearest evidence concerns fiber itself. In large analyses, people who eat a lot of fiber had less cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer. How much of this runs through the gut is not yet clear. Science also does not yet know what a “perfect” microbiome is. That is why this is not about lists of bacteria, but about what your gut gets to eat every day.

The good news: if you eat mostly plant-based and varied meals, you are doing your gut some good at the same time. Small adjustments are often enough, without bans.

Finally, a look at gut and brain: the two are in constant exchange, via nerves (especially the vagus nerve), the immune system and messenger substances from the bacteria. That is why nervousness sometimes hits the stomach, and the other way around, the gut reacts to stress. Whether diet also noticeably affects your everyday mood is only being studied in small, short studies, with mixed results. For ongoing low mood or anxiety, medical or therapeutic help is the right way, not diet alone.

Nerd factsStudies and numbers in detail

Fiber, numbers and state of the evidence

Reynolds et al. 2019 (Source 2) analyzes meta-analyses. In observational studies, mortality (overall and cardiovascular) as well as the occurrence of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer were 15 to 30% lower in the highest compared with the lowest fiber eaters. Clinical trials showed lower body weight, lower systolic blood pressure and lower total cholesterol.

Putting it in context: observational data show an association, not a cause. People who eat a lot of fiber often live healthier in other ways too.

On short-chain fatty acids (e.g. butyrate): Mann et al. 2024 (Source 18) describe in a review that they regulate the barrier function of the gut lining as well as immune defense in the mucosa and the body. This is mostly knowledge about mechanisms. In humans, according to a systematic review (Vinelli et al. 2022, Source 17), fiber does not consistently lead to a significant rise in short-chain fatty acids in stool in healthy adults. The effect depends on dose, type and structure of the fiber.

On the “healthy microbiome”: there is no consensus on a definition (Schmidt et al. 2018, Source 19). The DGE symposium 2021 (Source 20) states that the greatest possible diversity of gut bacteria and a high fiber intake can have a positive effect on gut health. At the DGE congress 2026, a speaker considered it possible that it matters less which microorganisms are present than which functions they fulfil (Source 24).

Gut-brain axis

Mechanisms: the review by Rusch et al. 2023 (Source 21) describes interactions in both directions. Stress acts on the gut-brain axis via the stress axis (HPA axis) and other pathways, and conversely the brain influences gut function and microbiota. Knowledge about the mechanisms comes mainly from studies with germ-free animals. How the gut flora acts on the brain in humans is not yet fully understood.

“Gut Feelings” study (Freijy et al. 2023, Source 22): randomized, placebo-controlled trial, 8 weeks, 119 non-clinical adults with moderate psychological distress and low intake of prebiotic fiber. The primary endpoint was total mood disturbance. A diet rich in prebiotics lowered it compared with placebo (d = −0.60; 95% confidence interval −1.18 to −0.03; p = 0.039). Anxiety, stress and sleep also improved. The confidence interval reaches almost to zero, and the group is small. Probiotics alone showed no detectable effect on mood (d = −0.19; p = 0.51), synbiotics no benefit (d = −0.03; p = 0.92). The authors consider larger studies necessary.

Hogenelst et al. 2025 (Source 23): exploratory sub-analysis of a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study in healthy people. 54 people completed it, 12 weeks of prebiotic fiber (acacia gum and carrot powder). Everyday mood improved in women, not in men. In the women, the decrease in negative mood was linked to more microbial diversity. “Exploratory” means: this was not the main aim of the study, so read it with caution.

Possible reasons for the differing results: baseline diet, type and dose of the fibers, duration, small groups, self-reporting, sex. There is no established explanation. The studies say nothing about whether diet can treat depression or anxiety disorders.

The short version

  • Fiber is the food of your gut bacteria and the best-supported lever: in large analyses it is associated with less cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. It shifts the mix of bacteria. In studies it mostly did not bring more diversity.
  • Yogurt, kefir and other fermented foods also change the gut flora. In the studies considered here, more diversity showed up only in one small study in which a broad selection was eaten.
  • Probiotic capsules: in healthy people there is no reliable evidence that they change the gut flora.

02What the science says

Three terms that often get mixed up

Prebiotics are substances that serve as food for your gut bacteria, for example certain fibers. Probiotics are live microorganisms, usually as a capsule or powder. Fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir or kimchi sit in between: they arise through deliberate fermentation and contain microorganisms, but they are not defined preparations. Milk itself is not fermented.

Fiber: food for the bacteria

If you eat more fiber, you mainly feed the bacteria that are considered beneficial (bifidobacteria and lactobacilli). In studies, the diversity in the gut mostly does not change as a result. So fiber shifts the mix, it does not automatically build a more “colorful” gut flora. Whether the shift brings you anything noticeable, the studies do not say.

The association between fiber and lower disease risk was clearest at 25 to 29 g per day. The DGE recommends at least 30 g. Most adults in Germany do not reach that: the median was 18 g (women) and 19 g (men) per day. This comes from the National Nutrition Survey II (2005 to 2007). According to the Max Rubner Institute, there are no newer representative data. Important: these are mostly associations, not proven causes.

In a large citizen-science study, people who ate more than 30 different plants per week had, on average, a more diverse gut than people with fewer than 10. Plants include fruit, vegetables, legumes, nuts and whole grains. This is an association, not yet proof. The 30 is also not a target, but the upper end of a comparison group.

Fermented foods: yogurt, kefir and co.

In one study, healthy adults ate a lot of fermented foods for ten weeks. Afterwards their gut was more diverse, and inflammation markers in the blood fell. A broad selection was eaten: yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi and other fermented vegetables, vegetable brine drinks and kombucha. Whether the mix in particular mattered has not been tested separately by any study. Variety is an obvious idea, but not a proven advantage.

For dairy products alone, the picture is more sober. Yogurt, kefir and milk increased certain groups of bacteria in studies. More diversity mostly did not show up. And more bifidobacteria do not automatically mean more health.

Probiotic capsules

In healthy adults there is no reliable evidence that probiotic capsules change the composition of the gut flora. Whether they help in some other way, this body of studies does not say. After antibiotic treatment, in one small study they even delayed the recovery of the gut flora, including at the mucosa.

Putting it in context

Why do fiber studies hardly change diversity, while people with a varied diet have more diverse guts? Short study duration, baseline differences and confounding factors probably play a role. This is not certain. In addition: there is no generally accepted definition of a “healthy” microbiome, and proving cause and effect is very difficult in microbiome research.

Nerd factsStudies, numbers and evidence in detail

The definitions

Prebiotic according to the ISAPP consensus 2017: a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms and confers a health benefit (Source 14). Fermented foods according to the ISAPP consensus 2021: foods made through desired microbial growth and enzymatic conversion of food components (Source 10).

The meta-analysis in detail

Meta-analysis from 2018: 64 randomized controlled trials with a total of 2,099 healthy adults (Source 1). Fructans and galacto-oligosaccharides in particular increased bifidobacteria and lactobacilli. For alpha diversity there was no difference from control. Butyrate in stool rose, just at the edge of significance (lower confidence limit 0.00, p = 0.05). These are changes in stool, not a proven health benefit.

State of the evidence

Lancet series 2019 (Reynolds et al., Source 2): the certainty of the evidence for fiber was rated moderate according to GRADE. DGE reference value: at least 30 g per day or at least 14.6 g per 1,000 kcal (Source 3). For intake in Germany, see the scientific assessment by the Max Rubner Institute (Source 5, as of June 2026): median intake 18 g (women) and 19 g (men) per day according to the National Nutrition Survey II, survey period November 2005 to January 2007.

American Gut in detail

Observational study “American Gut” (Source 6). In each case about 40 people were compared (42 with high, 43 with low plant diversity). The number of plants per week was based on self-reports in the survey. An additional, validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was completed by only 14.8% of participants. People with between 10 and 30 plants were not considered in this analysis.

Stanford study, dairy review, kefir study

Stanford (Source 7): randomized study with 36 healthy adults (18 per group), 17 weeks in total, of which 10 weeks of intervention. Fermented-food group: diversity rose steadily, inflammation markers fell. Fiber group: diversity stable. The primary endpoint (cytokine response score) remained unchanged. Small, short study.

Dairy products (Source 13): systematic review, 8 RCTs, 468 participants in total. Except for one study, none saw a change in bacterial diversity; in one it fell after milk consumption. Three studies showed higher lactobacilli and bifidobacteria after milk, yogurt or kefir. The authors consider the health relevance unclear.

Kefir (Source 15): randomized study with 28 healthy 18- to 30-year-olds (kefir 13, milk 9, yogurt 6), 150 mL daily over 2 weeks. Kefir increased lactate-producing bacteria, including Bifidobacterium breve. Very small and short, to be read only as an indication.

The reviews

Systematic review of 7 RCTs with 21 to 81 people per study (Source 8): no effect on alpha diversity, species richness or evenness. One study found a change in beta diversity. Only the composition was examined, not the health benefit. After antibiotics: Suez et al. 2018 (Source 9), small study with mucosal samples.

Evidence

Lack of a consensus definition of a normal stool microbiota: Kristensen et al. 2016 (Source 8) and Schmidt et al. 2018 (Source 19). On the difficulty of establishing causality: consensus statement 2025 (Source 11).

03How you could put it into practice

Every body, every daily life and every history is different. The following suggestions are meant in general terms. What suits you can only be found out individually.

  • Fiber in everyday life. About 30 g add up over the day with, for example, pumpernickel, a pear, whole-grain pasta, peas and bell pepper.
  • Increase slowly, drink enough. If you suddenly eat much more fiber, you are more likely to get bloating (Source 12).
  • Variety instead of perfection. If it fits into your day, now and then try something new, such as a different legume or type of nut.
  • Fermented foods, if you like them. You could try different kinds, such as yogurt, kefir or fermented vegetables, instead of always just one. Start with small amounts, because at the beginning bloating or soft stools are possible, which usually improves (Source 16). If you have a weakened immune system, serious illnesses or bowel conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome or IBD, talk to your doctor beforehand; in pregnancy, follow medical advice. If you react sensitively to histamine, pay attention to your body.
  • Probiotic capsules. For healthy adults there is so far no reliable evidence that they change the composition of the gut flora. If you take antibiotics or have pre-existing conditions, talk to your doctor beforehand.
Nerd factsThe calculation example

Calculation example from the DGE, not a study result (Source 4, calculated with DGExpert): 1 slice of pumpernickel (40 g) 3.5 g, 1 pear (140 g) 3.9 g, 1 portion of whole-grain pasta (125 g) 6.4 g, 1 portion of peeled peas (125 g) 11.8 g, 1 portion of steamed red bell pepper (150 g) 5.7 g. Total: 3.5 + 3.9 + 6.4 + 11.8 + 5.7 = 31.3 g.

Your starting point: Pick one or two things that feel easy. For example, swap a bread for a whole-grain bread or now and then try a different kind of yogurt or kefir.
