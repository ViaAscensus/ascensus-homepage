Nutrition Before Menopause

Prepare now, don’t catch up later:
What matters now for nutrition, bones and muscles

Job, family, sport: you do a lot, and still you sometimes feel held back, as if body and mind were running at half speed? Before menopause it is worth looking at muscles and bones and at what your food actually delivers, especially for iron and calcium.

Patrick SpenglerCertified Nutrition Coach
November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

The short version

  • In a large study, bone loss only picked up speed about a year before the final menstrual period. The years before are your chance: with protein and calcium from food, and with training as a supplement.
  • In the lab, calcium as well as coffee and tea clearly reduced iron absorption. In everyday life the effect was mostly smaller, and vitamin C helped.
  • You do not have to cut anything out. Small adjustments in everyday life can help. Which ones suit you is individual. Whether your iron is too low is something a blood test at the doctor’s can show.

01What this is about

You are not a couch potato. You organize job, family and daily life, keep everything running and do sport. And still you sometimes feel as if body and mind are running at half speed. Your energy fluctuates, and the scale does not move or even goes in the “wrong” direction, even though you are doing “everything right”.

Then you are not weak, and you are not automatically doing something wrong. Perhaps your body is sending different signals. Fatigue has many causes, and in the end a doctor is the one to clarify them. Nutrition is, however, a lever you hold in your own hands.

Because in the years before menopause, a lot is happening in the background that the scale does not show: muscles and bones become more important. And it is not only what you eat that counts, but also what of it arrives in the body. A coffee with a meal, or cheese with an iron-rich dish, can slow iron absorption, while vitamin C can support it. The good news: small adjustments in everyday life are often enough, with no bans at all.

One thing upfront: many studies are small, short or purely observational. This is about nutrition and daily life, not about hormone therapy or the treatment of symptoms. That belongs in medical hands.

02What the science says

Bones: the starting point counts

The SWAN study has followed women through menopause since 1996. Bone loss was greatest from about one year before to about two years after the final menstrual period. In the early phase with an irregular cycle, hardly any loss was measurable (Greendale 2012). Women with lower lumbar spine bone density before menopause had more fractures later (451 women, Shieh 2021). That is an association, not proof. But it speaks for building a good cushion beforehand.

Muscles: mirror instead of scale

At the beginning of the transition, the increase in fat mass doubled, and fat-free mass, mainly muscle, decreased. Body weight did not rise faster in the process (Greendale 2019). In the mirror, your body can therefore change: softer, less firm, more belly, while the scale does not jump. That is our conclusion from the measurements, not a mirror study.

Training and calcium

In a meta-analysis (premenopausal women, training studies of at least 24 weeks), training improved bone density at the femoral neck and lumbar spine (Kelley 2013). Strength training with jumping or impact loading did best, and the gains were small (Martyn-St James 2010). An older analysis with 143 women saw no difference for strength training alone (Kelley 2004).

Dairy products can also be worthwhile. In a small study from 1990, more calcium from dairy products slowed bone loss in the spine over three years (women aged 30 to 42, 20 versus 17 controls, Baran 1990). The DGE (German Nutrition Society) recommends 1,000 mg of calcium per day for adults. A worked example from the DGE portion table shows what that can look like: 250 ml milk (295 mg), 1 slice of Gouda (30 g, 287 mg), 1 cup of yogurt (150 g, 171 mg), 200 g broccoli (174 mg) and 1 large bowl of arugula (70 g, 112 mg) add up to roughly 1,040 mg. Without dairy it works too, for example with kale: 200 g provide 358 mg on their own.

Your body makes vitamin D mainly in the skin, and food contributes only little. According to the BfR (German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment), a general recommendation for supplements in healthy people cannot be justified. Whether your level is fine can only be shown by a blood test.

Iron: who slows whom?

The DGE recommends 16 mg of iron per day for women who menstruate, and 11 mg for men and women who do not. A worked example from nutrient tables (approximation): 60 g oats (2.2 mg), 2 slices of whole-grain bread (100 g together, 3.1 mg), 60 g dry lentils (3.6 mg), 100 g beef (around 3 mg) and 25 g pumpkin seeds (2 mg) add up to around 14 mg. So it is possible, but not on the side. Milk and cheese contribute almost nothing: 200 ml milk or 30 g cheese contain less than 0.1 mg. Iron from animal foods is also better absorbed than from plant foods.

What slowed absorption in the lab:

  • Calcium: 165 mg of calcium (as milk, cheese or calcium chloride) reduced iron absorption by 50 to 60% in 126 people, including iron from meat (Hallberg 1991). 165 mg is a good half slice of Gouda or a small glass of milk (around 140 ml, worked example from the DGE table). Milk, milkshake or cheese with a meal were in the same range (Hallberg 1992).
  • Coffee and tea: With a standard meal, tea reduced absorption by 62%, coffee by 35%, and orange juice increased it by 85% (Hallberg and Rossander 1982). Black tea inhibited by 79 to 94%, peppermint tea by 84%. Milk in tea or coffee changed little (Hurrell 1999). So herbal tea is not automatically harmless either.
  • Distance: Twelve healthy women (average 24.8 years) ate oatmeal with labeled iron three times: once with water, once with tea alongside, once with tea one hour later. The gap halved the inhibition from 37.2 to 18.1% (Ahmad Fuzi 2017).
  • Whole grains and vitamin C: Phytates in whole grains and legumes also slowed absorption. Vitamin C canceled this out in studies (Siegenberg 1991, 199 people) and worked mainly by breaking down this inhibition (Hallberg 1986, 299 people).

Putting it in perspective

These were single meals in the lab with labeled iron and small groups. With a normal everyday diet, the effects were clearly weaker (Cook and Reddy 2001, 12 people, five days). With longer-term higher calcium intake, a review found no worse iron levels in the blood, so the body apparently compensates (Lönnerdal 2010). In an eight-week study with 64 premenopausal women, three servings of low-fat milk per day also did not worsen iron status further (Faghih and Hedayati 2015).

For coffee and tea the picture is mixed. A review found an association with low iron status only when stores were low (Temme 2002). In Korea, coffee went along with lower ferritin in premenopausal women (ferritin shows stored iron). In a Japanese study with over 10,000 adults, it was only green tea in these women (Nanri 2023). That is observation, not cause, and why the results differ is an open question. So the studies do not show that milk or coffee lead to iron deficiency in you. The higher need due to menstruation is more important.

03How you could put it into practice

These are general ideas, not rules. Every body, every daily routine and every history is different. See what fits you, and adapt it to your life, not the other way around. As inspiration:

  1. Protein: A protein source in your main meals can help. How much makes sense for you is in the article “Protein: How Much You Actually Need”.
  2. Calcium: Where could a bit more calcium fit into your day? For example yogurt at breakfast, cheese on bread, broccoli, kale or arugula as a side. If you do not like or tolerate dairy, there are plant-based ways, such as the vegetables mentioned or calcium-rich mineral water.
  3. Combine iron with vitamin C: For example lentils with bell pepper, oats with berries or orange, a glass of orange juice with the meal.
  4. Coffee and tea: If it fits your day, you can drink them about an hour after the meal instead of with it. If not, that is fine too. Coffee is not the enemy. The rule is: “The dose makes the poison.”
  5. Spread out instead of giving up: The iron source at lunch, the cheese sandwich in the evening. You do not have to give up either one, you need both.
  6. Strength: Training belongs here, even though this is about nutrition. You could plan regular strength training, over months and, if it suits you, with some jumping or taking the stairs. In the studies, the programs ran for at least 24 weeks. How often, how intensively and with which exercises you train depends on your starting point, for example on joints, pre-existing conditions and time. It is best to get guidance, for example from a trainer.
  7. Supplements and medication: If you take any, ask at the pharmacy or your doctor about interactions and spacing.

Your starting point: Not sure where to begin? Then pick one or two things that feel easy in your daily life, for example coffee after breakfast instead of with it, or an orange with a lentil dish. A skipped day is just a data point, not a defeat that brings the whole house of cards down.

What makes sense for you depends on your daily life, your preferences and your starting point. That cannot be said in general terms, only looked at individually.

If you have persistent fatigue, changed or very heavy periods, or if you wonder whether your iron is too low, have it checked by a doctor (blood count, ferritin). The same applies to questions about bone density, vitamin D or hormone therapy.

The ASCENSUS View
